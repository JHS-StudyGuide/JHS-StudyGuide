Conditional Statements & Reasoning

A complete guide on transforming conditional statements, the two core modes of reasoning, the S.M.A.R.T. goal framework, and the two classical paths of inference.

1. Conditional Statements

Given a conditional $p \rightarrow q$, three related statements can be formed by switching and/or negating the hypothesis ($p$) and conclusion ($q$).

01 $q \rightarrow p$

Converse

Rule: Switch the hypothesis and conclusion.

Switching the hypothesis and conclusion does not preserve the original truth value.
02 $\sim p \rightarrow \sim q$

Inverse

Rule: Negate both the hypothesis and conclusion.

Formed by adding "not" (represented by "~") to each part of the original statement.
03 $\sim q \rightarrow \sim p$

Contrapositive

Rule: Negate the statement, then switch the hypothesis and conclusion.

Shares the same truth value as the Inverse.
04 Same Truth Value

Equivalent Statement

Rule: Two statements that always share the same truth value.

A conditional and its contrapositive are equivalent statements to one another.

2. Logical & Analytical Reasoning

Reasoning is thinking logically and analytically to work through a situation and reach a solution.

Logical Thinking

Analyzing situations using reasons to arrive at a solution.

  • Works step by step from known reasons
  • Builds toward a solution through justified steps

Analytical Thinking

Identifying and defining problems in order to reach a solution.

  • Starts by breaking down and defining the problem
  • Focuses on identifying the real issue before solving

Goal-Setting Criteria

4. Primary & Secondary Data

Data used in reasoning and research can be classified by where it originates.

Primary Data

  • Data that comes straight from the source
  • Collected firsthand for a specific purpose

Secondary Data

  • Based on others' research and data
  • Drawn from existing studies or records

5. Inductive vs. Deductive Reasoning

The two classical paths for moving between specific observations and general conclusions.

Inductive Reasoning (Sir Francis Bacon)

Direction: Specific → General

  • Based on observed patterns
  • Conclusion is called a conjecture — an unproven statement based on observation
  • Proving a conjecture requires showing it holds true in all cases

Deductive Reasoning (Aristotle)

Direction: General → Specific

  • Based on fact and definition
  • Arrives at a conclusion based on generalization
