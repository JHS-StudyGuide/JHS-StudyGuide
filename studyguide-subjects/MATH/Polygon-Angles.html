Polygons

Comprehensive overview of polygon properties, diagonals, angle relationships, and biological applications.

1. Definitions & Classifications

A polygon is a closed two-dimensional geometric figure formed by a finite number of straight line segments connected end-to-end. Polygons can be broadly classified by their angle configurations:

  • Convex Polygons: No interior angle is greater than 180°, and all diagonals lie entirely inside the figure.
  • Concave Polygons: At least one interior angle is strictly greater than 180°, causing at least one diagonal or line segment between internal points to pass outside the figure's boundary.
  • Equilateral Polygon: All sides of the polygon are congruent or equal.
  • Equiangular Polygon: All angles of the polygon are congruent or equal.
  • Regular Polygon: The polygon is convex, equilateral, and equiangular.
  • Irregular Polygon: The polygon is missing one or more properties of a regular polygon.
  • Vertex: The intersection of two adjacent sides/lines of a polygon.
  • Interior Angles: The adjacent lines of a polygon forming an angle which reside inside the lines of said polygon.
  • Exterior Angles: The adjacent lines of a polygon forming an angle which reside inside the lines of said polygon.
  • Diagonals: A line within the walls of a polygon touching two non-consecutive vertexes.

2. Calculation of Diagonals

A diagonal is a line segment connecting any two non-adjacent vertices of a polygon. Because a single vertex cannot draw a line to itself or its two immediately adjacent neighbors without overlapping the perimeter sides, the number of diagonals from a single vertex is always:

Diagonals from One Vertex = s - 3

To find the total number of unique diagonals (d) across the entire s-sided polygon without exhaustive drawing, we account for every vertex and divide by 2 (to prevent double-counting):

d = [s(s - 3)] / 2

Example (Octagon, s = 8): d = [8(8 - 3)] / 2 = (8 × 5) / 2 = 20 total diagonals.

Algebraic Decoding: If a polygon has d = 54 diagonals, solve for s using quadratic equations: 54 = [s(s - 3)] / 2 ⇒ 108 = s² - 3s ⇒ s² - 3s - 108 = 0. Factoring yields (s - 12)(s + 9) = 0, meaning the polygon has 12 sides.

3. Interior & Exterior Angle Relationships

Interior Angles Sum Theorem: Any s-sided polygon can be partitioned into exactly (s - 2) internal triangles from a single vertex. Since each triangle contributes 180°, the total interior angle sum (Si) depends solely on side count:

Si = (s - 2) × 180°

In a regular polygon (where all sides and angles are equal), each individual interior angle is divided equally among all vertices: m∠i = [(s - 2) × 180°] / s.

Sum of Exterior Angles Invariance: Regardless of how complex, large, or concave a polygon is, the sum of its exterior angles (one per vertex) is strictly invariant and always completes a full rotation:

Sum of Exterior Angles = 360°

In a regular polygon, each exterior angle is simply: m∠e = 360° / s. Furthermore, at any given vertex, the interior and exterior angles are supplementary: m∠i + m∠e = 180°.

4. Polygon Area via Apothem

The area of a regular polygon connects one-dimensional lineal measurements to planar space. By drawing segments from the center of the polygon to each vertex, the figure divides into equal isosceles triangles.

Using the apothem (a)—the perpendicular distance from the center to the midpoint of any side—and the side length (s) across (n) total sides, the area formula is:

Area = 1/2 × a × n × s

Note: Since Perimeter (P) = n × s, this formula is frequently simplified in architectural and engineering design as Area = 1/2 × a × P.

5. Real-Life Applications: Nature & Biology

Abstract geometric properties frequently manifest as structural adaptations in biology and natural architecture. A primary example is the compound eye of a housefly, which is packed with microscopic corneal lenses called facets.

Each facet is shaped as a regular hexagon (s = 6). Applying our interior angle formula demonstrates why this shape tiles so efficiently without wasted interstitial space:

m∠i = [(6 - 2) × 180°] / 6 = (4 × 180°) / 6 = 120°

Because three hexagonal vertices meet perfectly at a point (120° × 3 = 360°), hexagons provide optimal mechanical stability and surface area maximization in biological structures.

6. Truth Value and Conditional Statements

Truth Value is how true a statement is according to its hypothesis and conclusion. A Conditional statement is a statement holding a hypothesis and conclusion (if, then statement)

  • Hypothesis (if): This is the condition of the conclusion. The truth value of the hypothesis does NOT matter. Represented by "p".
  • Conclusion (then): This is the result of the hypothesis. If the conclusion is false, the entire statement is false unless the hypothesis is also false. Represented by "q".
  • Counter-example: This is the example wherein it disproves the conclusion. If a statement has no counter-example, it is true.
  • p > q: This simply means "hypothesis in relation to the conclusion".
