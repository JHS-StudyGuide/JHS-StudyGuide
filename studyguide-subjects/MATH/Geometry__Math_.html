Part 1

Basic Geometry

The undefined terms, the pieces you can cut a line into, and the rules for measuring and splitting a segment.

1.1

Point, Line & Plane

These three ideas are Geometry's undefined terms — they're described using everyday language rather than formally defined, because every other definition in Geometry is eventually built out of them.

Point

An exact location with no size — no length, width, or thickness. Represented by a dot and named with a single capital letter.

Notation: point P
Line

A straight path of points with infinite length but no width, extending forever in two directions.

Notation: AB with a double-arrow over it, or a lowercase script letter like line m
Plane

A perfectly flat surface with infinite length and width but no thickness — think of it extending forever in every direction.

Notation: plane ABC (3 points), or plane M
Real-world stand-ins

A pinpoint of light is close to a point. A tightly stretched wire suggests a line. A calm lake's surface suggests a plane. None are perfect — real objects always have some size — but they're useful mental pictures.

A plane needs three noncollinear points (points not all on one line) to be named and pinned down — two points could sit on infinitely many different planes, but three points that aren't in a row determine exactly one flat surface through them.

1.3

Betweenness & Collinearity

Before you can say a point "lies between" two others, two conditions have to hold at once — not just that it looks like it's in the middle.

Collinear points

Points that lie on the same line are collinear. Points that don't all fit on one line are noncollinear. Any two points are automatically collinear (a line can always be drawn through two points) — collinearity only becomes a meaningful question once you have three or more points.

Betweenness

Point B is between points A and C if and only if:

  1. A, B, and C are distinct, collinear points, and
  2. The distances satisfy AB + BC = AC.
B is between A and C ⟺ A, B, C collinear and AB + BC = AC

This means B sits somewhere on segment AC, and the two smaller pieces add up exactly to the whole. Try dragging point B outside segment AC in the ruler above — the verdict flips to "not between," because the addition no longer works, even though the three points are still collinear.

Ruler Postulate: every point on a line matches exactly one real number (its coordinate), and distance between two points is the absolute value of the difference of their coordinates: d(P, Q) = |xP − xQ|. That's the rule the ruler widget is running every time you drag a point.
1.4

Segment Addition Postulate

The same rule as betweenness, but used in the other direction — to build up an unknown length instead of testing one.

If B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC. The postulate also works backward: if the equation holds, B must be between A and C. That two-way property is what makes it usable in proofs, not just an observation.

B between A and C ⟺ AB + BC = AC

Worked example: if AB = 9 and BC = 14, and B is between A and C, then AC = 9 + 14 = 23. Flip it around: if AC = 23 and AB = 9, then BC = 23 − 9 = 14.

Algebra example: B is between A and C. AB = 2x + 1, BC = x + 5, AC = 30. Since AB + BC = AC: (2x + 1) + (x + 5) = 30 → 3x + 6 = 30 → x = 8. So AB = 17 and BC = 13 (and 17 + 13 = 30 ✓).

1.5

Congruent Segments

Congruent just means "same length" — position and direction don't matter at all.

AB ≅ CD ⟺ AB = CD

Note the symbols: AB (no bar, or written "AB") refers to the length, a number — so you can write AB = CD. seg. AB refers to the actual figure — so you write seg. AB ≅ seg. CD, using the congruence symbol, not the equals sign, since a figure can't literally equal another figure.

Tick marks (identical small hash marks drawn across two segments in a diagram) are how a figure shows two segments are congruent without stating numbers. If two segments both measure 6 cm, they're congruent even if one is horizontal and the other is vertical, or they're on opposite sides of the page.

1.6

Midpoint of a Segment

A midpoint has to satisfy both betweenness and equal distance — it's a special case built from ideas you already have.

M is the midpoint of segment AB if and only if:

  1. M is between A and B, and
  2. AM = MB.
M is the midpoint of AB ⟺ M is between A and B, and AM = MB

M splits AB into two congruent segments: seg. AM ≅ seg. MB. On a number line, the midpoint's coordinate is the average of the two endpoint coordinates.

xM = (xA + xB) / 2
1.7

Practice

Try each one before revealing the solution. Full working shown, step by step.

1. Points X, Y, Z are collinear, in that order (Y is between X and Z). Name: (a) the segment with endpoints X and Z, (b) the ray that starts at Y and passes through Z, (c) a pair of opposite rays with endpoint Y.

(a) segment XZ — order doesn't matter for a segment, so "segment ZX" is also correct. (b) ray YZ — the endpoint (Y) is always named first for a ray. (c) ray YX and ray YZ — they share endpoint Y and point in exactly opposite directions along the same line, since Y is between X and Z.

2. On a number line, A is at −3 and C is at 10. B is between A and C, and AB = 6. Find BC and confirm the Segment Addition Postulate.

Step 1 — Find AC using the Ruler Postulate: AC = |x_A − x_C| = |−3 − 10| = 13 Step 2 — Apply the Segment Addition Postulate: AB + BC = AC 6 + BC = 13 BC = 13 − 6 = 7 Step 3 — Check: AB + BC = 6 + 7 = 13 = AC ✓

3. M is the midpoint of segment PQ. PM = 3x + 2 and MQ = 5x − 8. Find x, then find PM.

Step 1 — Midpoint means PM = MQ: 3x + 2 = 5x − 8 Step 2 — Solve for x: 2 + 8 = 5x − 3x 10 = 2x x = 5 Step 3 — Substitute back into PM: PM = 3(5) + 2 = 17 Check MQ = 5(5) − 8 = 17 — matches PM ✓

4. Points D, E, F lie on a line with coordinates 4, 9, and 17. Is E between D and F? Justify using the definition of betweenness.

Step 1 — Check collinearity: All three points are given as lying on the same line ✓ Step 2 — Compute distances: DE = |4 − 9| = 5 EF = |9 − 17| = 8 DF = |4 − 17| = 13 Step 3 — Test DE + EF = DF: 5 + 8 = 13 = DF ✓ Conclusion: Since both conditions of betweenness are satisfied, E is between D and F.

Angles

What an angle is made of, how to classify one, and the six relationships that connect two angles to each other.

2.1

Parts of an Angle

An angle is formed by two rays sharing an endpoint. Drag the arm below to see the vertex, sides, and interior — plus how the classification name changes as the measure changes.

Vertex

The common endpoint shared by the two rays — where the angle "hinges."

Sides

The two rays themselves, e.g. ray BA and ray BC form ∠ABC.

Interior

The region "inside" the opening between the two sides.

Naming

∠ABC or ∠CBA (vertex letter always in the middle), or just ∠B if there's no ambiguity, or ∠1 if labeled with a number in a diagram.

2.2

Classifying Angle Pairs

Six relationships show up constantly in Geometry proofs. Three of them (complementary, supplementary, congruent) are only about the measures — the angles don't even need to touch. The other three (adjacent, vertical, linear pair) are about position.

Complementary

Two angles whose measures sum to 90°. Don't need to be adjacent.

m∠A + m∠B = 90°
Supplementary

Two angles whose measures sum to 180°. Don't need to be adjacent.

m∠A + m∠B = 180°
Congruent angles

Two angles with the same measure, marked with matching arcs in a diagram.

∠A ≅ ∠B ⟺ m∠A = m∠B
Adjacent angles

Two angles that share a vertex and a side, with no overlapping interior.

e.g. ∠ABC & ∠CBD share vertex B, side BC
Vertical angles

The pair of non-adjacent angles opposite each other where two lines cross. Always congruent.

Vertical Angles Theorem: always ≅
Linear pair

Two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line. Always supplementary.

m∠1 + m∠2 = 180°, and adjacent
Two common mix-ups: every linear pair is supplementary, but not every supplementary pair is a linear pair (they might not even touch). Likewise, every pair of vertical angles is congruent, but congruent angles don't have to be vertical angles.

2.3

Practice

Same drill as Part 1 — work it out first, then reveal.

1. ∠A and ∠B are complementary. m∠A = 3x + 5 and m∠B = 2x. Find x, then find each angle's measure.

Step 1 — Complementary means the measures sum to 90°: (3x + 5) + 2x = 90 Step 2 — Solve for x: 5x + 5 = 90 5x = 85 x = 17 Step 3 — Substitute back: m∠A = 3(17) + 5 = 56° m∠B = 2(17) = 34° Check: 56 + 34 = 90 ✓

2. Two angles form a linear pair. One measures (4x − 10)° and the other measures (2x + 40)°. Find x and both measures.

Step 1 — A linear pair is always supplementary: (4x − 10) + (2x + 40) = 180 Step 2 — Solve for x: 6x + 30 = 180 6x = 150 x = 25 Step 3 — Substitute back: Angle 1 = 4(25) − 10 = 90° Angle 2 = 2(25) + 40 = 90° Check: 90 + 90 = 180 ✓ — interesting: a linear pair can be two right angles at once (that's exactly what happens when the two lines are perpendicular).

3. Two lines intersect, forming vertical angles ∠1 and ∠3. m∠1 = (5x + 12)° and m∠3 = (7x − 8)°. Find x, then find the measures of all four angles formed.

Step 1 — Vertical angles are congruent: 5x + 12 = 7x − 8 Step 2 — Solve for x: 12 + 8 = 7x − 5x 20 = 2x x = 10 Step 3 — Find ∠1 and ∠3: m∠1 = 5(10) + 12 = 62° m∠3 = 7(10) − 8 = 62° ✓ matches ∠1 Step 4 — ∠2 and ∠4 form linear pairs with ∠1 and ∠3, so they're supplementary to 62°: m∠2 = 180 − 62 = 118° m∠4 = 118° (vertical to ∠2) All four angles: 62°, 118°, 62°, 118° — going around the intersection.
