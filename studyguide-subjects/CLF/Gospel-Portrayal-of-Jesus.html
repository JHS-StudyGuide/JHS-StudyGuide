The Gospels' Portrayal of Jesus

Why four Gospels exist, how they differ, and the evangelist symbols used to remember them — condensed from a long, dense reading into a quick-scan reviewer.

Why four accounts? People are understood more fully through multiple points of view — the four Gospels do the same for Jesus, each giving a fuller, more complete picture of who He is.
Synoptic Gospels Matthew, Mark, and Luke are the "synoptics" — from Greek for "to see together" — because they share enough similarities to be studied side by side.
John is different Longer, more poetic, and containing material not found in the synoptics — best compared using the Last Supper accounts (Mt 26, Mk 14, Lk 22 vs. Jn 13).

3. The Gospel Symbols

Early Christians, many of whom couldn't read, remembered the evangelists through symbols drawn from Ezekiel 1:10 and Revelation 4:7 — a vision of four living creatures: a lion, a calf/ox, a human, and an eagle. Each symbol matches how that Gospel opens.

✎

Matthew — the Man

His Gospel opens with a genealogy — a record of Jesus' human ancestors going back to Abraham.

🦁

Mark — the Lion

His Gospel opens with John the Baptist "crying out in the desert" — a lion is associated with the desert, and the symbol also fits Mark's vivid, direct style.

🐂

Luke — the Ox

His Gospel opens with Zechariah offering a sacrifice in the temple — the ox recalls ancient animal sacrifice.

🦅

John — the Eagle

His Gospel opens with a soaring prologue praising Jesus — like an eagle, said to be the only creature able to look straight at the sun (or, here, the face of God).

4. Lesson Insights

Four Gospels exist so we can know Jesus from four angles rather than one — the way we understand a person better through many relationships, not just one.

Matthew, Mark, and Luke (the synoptics) can be studied side by side because of their similarities; John stands apart in length, tone, and content, and leans hardest into Jesus' divinity.

The strongest ideas to remember: why four Gospels exist, what makes the synoptics "synoptic," each evangelist's one-line portrait of Jesus, and which symbol goes with which Gospel.

5. The Joy of the Gospel

"The Gospel, radiant with the glory of Christ's cross, constantly invites us to rejoice." — Evangelii Gaudium, 5

We're blessed to be able to study the Gospels — but the real question is how we read them so that we actually meet the Risen Christ, rather than just hearing familiar words. The Parable of the Sower (Mt 13:1-9, explained in Mt 13:18-23) gives us a way to check our own response to God's Word.

The Parable, in short: Jesus sat by the sea and taught a large crowd from a boat. A sower scattered seed on four kinds of ground — the path, rocky ground, thorny ground, and rich soil — and only the seed on rich soil grew and produced fruit, "a hundredfold, sixtyfold, or thirtyfold."

The sower is Jesus, and the seed is the Word of God. The four types of soil represent four ways people respond to that Word. Tap one below.

Our response to the Gospel depends on the condition of our heart — a hardened or indifferent heart struggles to receive God's Word with joy, so the Word can't bear fruit in it. To be the "good soil" is to hear, understand, receive, and let the Word of God work in our lives — and that is where true joy in the Gospel is found.
