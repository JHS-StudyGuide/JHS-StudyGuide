01

Betweenness of Points

Before you can say a point "lies between" two others, two conditions have to hold at once — not just that it looks like it's in the middle.

Point B is between points A and C if and only if:

  1. A, B, and C are distinct points on the same line (they're collinear).
  2. The distances satisfy AB + BC = AC.
B is between A and C ⟺ AB + BC = AC

This means B sits somewhere on segment AC, and the two smaller pieces add up exactly to the whole. Try dragging point B outside segment AC in the ruler above — you'll see the verdict flip to "not between," because the addition no longer works.

02

The Ruler Postulate

This is the postulate that lets you turn a line into a set of numbers you can actually calculate with.

Every point on a line corresponds to exactly one real number, and every real number corresponds to exactly one point — a one-to-one correspondence. The distance between two points is just the absolute value of the difference between their numbers:

Distance between P and Q = | xP − xQ |

That's literally what the ruler widget above is computing every time you drag a point. The absolute value matters because distance can't be negative — |3 − 7| and |7 − 3| both give 4.

03

Segment Addition Postulate

The same rule as betweenness, but used in the other direction — to build up an unknown length instead of testing one.

If B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC. And the postulate also works backward: if the equation holds, B must be between A and C. That two-way property is what makes it a postulate you can use for proofs, not just an observation.

B between A and C ⟺ AB + BC = AC

Worked example: if AB = 9 and BC = 14, and B is between A and C, then AC = 9 + 14 = 23. Flip it around: if AC = 23 and AB = 9, then BC = 23 − 9 = 14.

04

Congruent Segments

Congruent just means "same length" — position and direction don't matter at all.

AB ≅ CD ⟺ AB = CD

The tick marks (like one small hash mark on both segments in a diagram) are how a figure tells you two segments are congruent without stating the numbers. If two segments both measure 6 cm, they're congruent even if one is horizontal and the other is vertical, or one is on the left side of the page and the other is on the right.

05

Midpoint of a Segment

A midpoint has to satisfy both betweenness and equal distance — it's a special case built from ideas you already have.

M is the midpoint of segment AB if and only if:

  1. M is between A and B, and
  2. AM = MB.
M is the midpoint of AB ⟺ M is between A and B, and AM = MB

M splits AB into two congruent segments: AM ≅ MB. On a number line, the midpoint's coordinate is just the average of the two endpoint coordinates.

xM = (xA + xB) / 2
06

Practice

Try each one before revealing the solution. Full working shown, step by step.

1. On a number line, A is at −3 and C is at 10. B is between A and C, and AB = 6. Find BC and confirm the Segment Addition Postulate.

Step 1 — Find AC using the Ruler Postulate: AC = |x_A − x_C| = |−3 − 10| = 13 Step 2 — Apply the Segment Addition Postulate: AB + BC = AC 6 + BC = 13 BC = 13 − 6 = 7 Step 3 — Check: AB + BC = 6 + 7 = 13 = AC ✓

2. M is the midpoint of segment PQ. PM = 3x + 2 and MQ = 5x − 8. Find x, then find PM.

Step 1 — Midpoint means PM = MQ: 3x + 2 = 5x − 8 Step 2 — Solve for x: 2 + 8 = 5x − 3x 10 = 2x x = 5 Step 3 — Substitute back into PM: PM = 3(5) + 2 = 17 Check MQ = 5(5) − 8 = 17 — matches PM ✓

3. Points D, E, F lie on a line with coordinates 4, 9, and 17. Is E between D and F? Justify using the definition of betweenness.

Step 1 — Check collinearity: All three points are given as being on the same line ✓ Step 2 — Compute distances: DE = |4 − 9| = 5 EF = |9 − 17| = 8 DF = |4 − 17| = 13 Step 3 — Test DE + EF = DF: 5 + 8 = 13 = DF ✓ Conclusion: Since both conditions of betweenness are satisfied, E is between D and F.
